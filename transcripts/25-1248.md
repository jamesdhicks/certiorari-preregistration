> ## Case Background
>
> **Rio Grande Foundation v. Toulouse Oliver (No. 25-1248)** is a First Amendment challenge to New Mexico's Campaign Reporting Act, which requires donor disclosure for "independent expenditures" including ads that merely *mention* a candidate within 30/60 days of an election. Rio Grande Foundation, a 501(c)(3) issue-advocacy group represented by the Liberty Justice Center, wanted to mail its legislative "Freedom Index" scorecard before the 2020 election but refrained due to the disclosure triggers. A divided Tenth Circuit panel upheld the law under exacting scrutiny ([opinion](https://www.ca10.uscourts.gov/sites/ca10/files/opinions/010110799219.pdf), [Justia summary](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca10/24-2070/24-2070-2025-09-09.html)), with Judge Eid dissenting and Judge Hartz concurring reluctantly; rehearing en banc was denied over a two-judge dissent.
>

> **Factors favoring denial:**
> - The petition's questions are largely **merits-driven rather than split-driven** — it asks the Court to extend *Buckley*'s major-purpose/express-advocacy limits and *Bonta*'s tailoring rule, and even to replace exacting scrutiny with strict scrutiny, but does not present a crisp circuit split. Courts of appeals have broadly upheld similar electioneering-communication disclosure regimes.
> - The Court has **repeatedly denied materially similar petitions** challenging state donor-disclosure laws for issue speech near elections (e.g., *Delaware Strong Families v. Denn* (2016, denied over dissent), *Independence Institute v. FEC* (summarily affirmed, 2017), *Gaspee Project v. Mederos* (denied 2022, a near-identical post-*Bonta* challenge to a state electioneering disclosure law, also with substantial amicus support)).
> - Disclosure requirements were upheld 8–1 in *Citizens United*, and the Court has consistently distinguished election-related disclosure (upheld) from the charitable-donor disclosure struck down in *Bonta*.
> - Historically only two or three justices have publicly signaled interest in revisiting this line — short of the four votes needed.

But the absence of a clean circuit split and the Court's consistent pattern of denying essentially identical challenges to state electioneering-disclosure laws — even after *Bonta* — make a denial the more likely outcome.
